## House style (from Damian's v2.0 revisions)

Apply to all copy on the site and in LinkedIn posts. Agreed with Damian on 11 October 2026.

1. **Qualify rather than assert.** Prefer 'many' to 'most', and 'may' where the evidence is not certain. Do not state what a regulator or insurer will do; describe what a firm may need to demonstrate.
2. **Write in full sentences.** Avoid fragments, rhetorical one liners and deliberately punchy endings. Captions and introductions should read as complete sentences.
3. **No hyphens in prose.** Write day to day, fee earner, medium sized, AI assisted, large scale, well designed, fixed fee, role based. Hyphens in block IDs and URLs are functional and remain.
4. **Measured, institutional vocabulary.** For example, 'consequences of failure are significant' rather than 'the cost of error is high', and 'governance arrangements' and 'appropriate supervision'.
5. **No wry or pointed lines.** Remove anything that could read as a criticism of the reader, of other suppliers or of generalists, and colloquial scene setting.
6. **Frame the ending as a transfer, not an exit.** Prefer 'effective transition' and 'transfer of knowledge, responsibility and capability' to 'end well', 'step away' or 'graduation'.
7. **Precise, legally careful wording.** For example, 'prohibited the relevant conduct' rather than 'prohibited it'.
8. **Lower case for spelt out terms.** For example, 'professional indemnity insurers'. Abbreviations such as PI, COLP and SRA remain in capitals.
9. **'Proportionate', never 'fractional'.** The first principle is 'Senior capability, proportionate commitment', and the same word is used wherever the idea appears elsewhere.
10. **Hero headline.** 'Service led digital delivery and secure AI for regulated organisations.'

### 2. The gap

**H2:** Where the exposure sits

**Intro:** Many firms have approved an enterprise AI tool and written a policy for using it. The harder part is making sure the two match day to day. Where they do not, three consequences may follow.

**Client confidentiality.** If a fee earner enters privileged material into an unapproved tool, the immediate concern is client confidentiality rather than technology alone. A breach is no less serious because a policy prohibited the relevant conduct.

**Regulatory scrutiny.** Firms must be able to demonstrate appropriate supervision of how work is produced, including work supported by AI. A firm may need to show that its controls operated in practice, not only that a policy was in place.

**Professional indemnity.** Professional indemnity insurers are beginning to take an interest in how firms govern AI use. Firms that can provide evidence of governance in practice may be better placed at renewal than those relying on a policy document alone.

### 3. Five questions

**H2:** Five questions your firm should be able to answer

1. Which AI tools are approved for use with client data, and how is that enforced?
2. How do you know that unapproved tools are not being used for client work?
3. Who reviews AI assisted output before it leaves the firm, and is that review recorded?
4. How are fee earners trained, and how often is that training refreshed?
5. If something went wrong, could you reconstruct what was done, by whom and with what data?

**Line beneath:** If any of these answers currently relies on the policy alone, that is the place to begin.

### Post 1: The policy and the desk (text)

Many law firms now have an AI policy. It will often have been drafted carefully, reviewed by risk leaders and approved through the firm's governance arrangements.

The harder question is what happens at the desk.

Which tools are fee earners actually using? What client material goes into them? Who checks the output before it reaches the client?

In many firms, these questions cannot yet be answered consistently and with evidence. The policy describes the intended position, but day to day practice may not be sufficiently visible.

That gap matters. A confidentiality breach is no less serious because a policy prohibited the relevant conduct. A firm may still need to demonstrate that work was appropriately supervised and that its controls operated in practice.

The useful work now is not writing another policy. It is ensuring that the existing policy, controls and day to day practice are aligned.

### Post 3: The constraint is rarely headcount (text)

In central government programmes, the common diagnosis is a shortage of people. The usual remedy is a larger supplier.

In my experience, the constraint is more often a shortage of experience in the right places. A decision waiting three weeks for an owner. A dependency nobody has the authority to resolve. A service ready to ship but held at an approval gate.

Additional headcount alone does not resolve these constraints. In some programmes, a small number of experienced practitioners with clear authority can have greater impact by accelerating decisions, resolving dependencies and helping services move through approval.

That is the case for smaller, more senior teams: lower fixed cost, faster mobilisation, and a direct line between the people funding change and the people delivering it.
